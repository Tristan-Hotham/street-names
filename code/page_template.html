High Street or Main Street?

Every named street in the United Kingdom, {{meta.n_streets|int}} of them, mapped by its name. Where does High Street give way to Main Street, and which street words belong to only one corner of the country?

Dr Tristan Hotham, Independent researcher · 2026 · Data: OS Open Names (Open Government Licence v3); OpenStreetMap (ODbL); ONS boundaries
{{meta.n_streets|int}}named streets: {{meta.n_gb|int}} in Great Britain (Ordnance Survey) and {{meta.n_ni|int}} in Northern Ireland (OpenStreetMap)
{{hm.uk.high|int}}High Streets, the commonest street name in the UK
{{hm.uk.main|int}}Main Streets, sixth. Main beats High in Scotland ({{hm.nation.Scotland.main|int}} to {{hm.nation.Scotland.high|int}}) and Northern Ireland ({{hm.nation.Northern Ireland.main|int}} to {{hm.nation.Northern Ireland.high|int}})
{{hm.by_kind_midlands_north.Village.main_share|pct}}of the High or Main Streets in East Midlands and Yorkshire villages are Main Streets; in their towns, {{hm.by_kind_midlands_north.Town.main_share|pct}}

Every town in Britain seems to have a High Street, and every village in Scotland a Main Street. Somewhere between the two, the name changes. Ordnance Survey publishes the name and position of every named road in Great Britain as open data, and OpenStreetMap's volunteers have mapped the streets of Northern Ireland, so the line can be drawn rather than guessed. I took all {{meta.n_streets|int}} named streets and sorted them by name, by the word at the end (Road, Lane, Close, Wynd) and by the word at the start (Heol, Back, Church).

The map below starts with the big question: in each 20 km square, how many of its High Streets and Main Streets are Main Streets? Then switch to the most common name in each square, pick any of {{n_terms|int}} street words to see where it lives, or type your own street's name and see where its namesakes are.

      02 · The divideHigh Street in the south, Main Street in the north, and in the villages

      Across the UK there are {{hm.uk.high|int}} High Streets and {{hm.uk.main|int}} Main Streets. High Street is the commonest street name in the country, and in England ({{hm.nation.England.high|int}}) and Wales ({{hm.nation.Wales.high|int}}, against just {{hm.nation.Wales.main|int}} Main Streets). Scotland has {{hm.nation.Scotland.main|int}} Main Streets to {{hm.nation.Scotland.high|int}} High Streets, and Northern Ireland {{hm.nation.Northern Ireland.main|int}} to {{hm.nation.Northern Ireland.high|int}}.

      But the divide is not a clean north and south. London has {{hm.region.London.high|int}} High Streets and one Main Street. The South West, the East of England and Wales all keep Main Street under 8% of the pair. The surprise is the middle of England: in the East Midlands Main Street wins, {{hm.region.East Midlands.main|int}} to {{hm.region.East Midlands.high|int}}, and in Yorkshire and the Humber {{hm.region.Yorkshire and the Humber.main|int}} to {{hm.region.Yorkshire and the Humber.high|int}}. The North East, closer to Scotland, has only {{hm.region.North East.main|int}} Main Streets.

      Main Street's share of each region's High and Main Streets
      Number of streets named exactly "Main Street" divided by those named "High Street" or "Main Street". England by region.

      Of England's {{hm.england_councils|int}} councils, {{hm.england_councils_main_more|int}} have more Main Streets than High Streets. Most lie in a belt from Leicestershire and Rutland through Nottinghamshire and Lincolnshire into Yorkshire and on to Cumbria: North Yorkshire ({{hm.england_main_top.0.1|int}} Main, {{hm.england_main_top.0.2|int}} High), the East Riding ({{hm.england_main_top.1.1|int}} to {{hm.england_main_top.1.2|int}}), Harborough ({{hm.england_main_top.2.1|int}} to {{hm.england_main_top.2.2|int}}) and Melton ({{hm.england_main_top.3.1|int}} to {{hm.england_main_top.3.2|int}}). In Scotland it is the other way round: only {{hm.scotland_high_n|int}} of {{hm.scotland_councils|int}} councils have more High Streets than Main Streets, led by Highland, East Lothian and Moray.

      A village word

      The clearest pattern is not about latitude at all. Ordnance Survey records the town or village each street belongs to, so I matched each street to that settlement's type. In England's towns, Main Street makes up {{hm.by_kind_england.Town.main_share|pct}} of the pair; in England's villages, {{hm.by_kind_england.Village.main_share|pct}}. In the East Midlands and Yorkshire the gap is wider still: {{hm.by_kind_midlands_north.Town.main_share|pct}} in towns, {{hm.by_kind_midlands_north.Village.main_share|pct}} in villages. Even in Scotland, towns lean to High Street more than villages do ({{hm.by_kind_scotland.Town.main_share|pct}} Main against {{hm.by_kind_scotland.Village.main_share|pct}}).

      Main Street's share, by size of place
      Streets matched to their Ordnance Survey town or village ({{hm.kind_matched_share|pct}} of Great Britain's streets matched). Cities are left out: they have too few of either.

      High Street is a town name. Main Street, across the Midlands, the north and Scotland, is a village name.

      This fits the usual reading of the names, though the map cannot prove it: "high" in High Street once meant chief or principal, as in high road, and the name was used for the main street of a market town; a village's one street was simply its main street. The data show where each name survives, not when it was given.

      04 · Regional wordsWynds, lokes, twittens and garths

      Most street names end in a handful of words. Road ends {{gen_road_pct|pct}} of all names, Close {{gen_close_pct|pct}}, Street {{gen_street_pct|pct}} and Lane {{gen_lane_pct|pct}}. The rare ones are the interesting ones, because many of them belong to one place.       The most regional street words
      Endings and first words used at least 150 times, ranked by concentration: how many times more common in their top region than across the UK. The three most concentrated per region are shown; the full list is in the data.
      Concentration is a location quotient: the region's share of all streets with the word divided by its share of all streets. Wales and Northern Ireland count as regions. Welsh first words reach about {{lq_wales_max|f1}}, the most possible, because Wales holds {{wales_share|pct1}} of the UK's streets and almost all of them.

      Some of the patterns are about history, some about landscape, and some about how a place was built. Drove ({{terms.r_drove.n|int}}) is the fen and marsh word for a track for driving stock, and {{terms.r_drove.top_councils.0.1|int}} are in East Cambridgeshire. Names ending in -gate as one word, or in Gate, number {{terms.r_gate.n|int}}; {{stories.gate_yorks_em|pct}} are in the north of England and the East Midlands, where gate in a street name is usually traced to the Old Norse word for a street, as in York's Micklegate. Bolton has {{terms.w_back.top_councils.0.1|int}} names starting with Back, the named back streets behind its terraces. In Northern Ireland, {{stories.park_ni|pct}} of streets end in Park, against {{stories.park_gb|pct1}} in Great Britain: the housing estate is a Park there, where in England it is a Close.

      05 · Other languagesHeol, Rathad and Bóthar

      Ordnance Survey records Welsh and Scottish Gaelic names alongside English ones. {{stories.welsh_any_n|int}} streets have a Welsh name or start with a Welsh street word, {{stories.welsh_any_wales|pct}} of the streets in Wales. Heol, road, is the most common ({{terms.cy_heol.n|int}}, with {{terms.cy_heol.top_councils.0.1|int}} in Carmarthenshire), then Ffordd ({{terms.cy_ffordd.n|int}}), Clos ({{terms.cy_clos.n|int}}) and Lôn ({{terms.cy_lon.n|int}}). Gwynedd has the most and Ceredigion the next highest share. Scottish Gaelic street names are far rarer in the data: {{stories.gaelic_n|int}}, mostly in the Western Isles and Highland. In Northern Ireland, {{terms.ga_any.n|int}} streets ({{stories.irish_ni_share|pct}}) carry an Irish name in OpenStreetMap, {{terms.ga_any.top_councils.0.1|int}} of them in Newry, Mourne and Down; that measures where mappers have added the bilingual signs, not where Irish is spoken.

      06 · The commonest namesStation Road, Church Lane and the king

      After High Street come Station Road ({{top_uk.1.1|int}}), Church Lane ({{top_uk.2.1|int}}), Church Street ({{top_uk.3.1|int}}) and Mill Lane ({{top_uk.4.1|int}}). Yet most names are rare: the UK's streets share {{meta.n_names|int}} different names, and {{meta.unique_share|pct}} of those names belong to one street only.

      Royalty is everywhere. Names with King or King's number {{royal.w_king|int}}, Queen or Queen's {{royal.w_queen|int}}, Victoria {{royal.w_victoria|int}}, George {{royal.w_george|int}} and Albert {{royal.w_albert|int}}. Celebrations leave their own traces: Jubilee {{royal.w_jubilee|int}}, Elizabeth {{royal.w_elizabeth|int}} and Coronation {{royal.w_coronation|int}}. Not every George or King is a monarch, of course; many are people or pubs.

      07 · Your councilEvery council's commonest and most characteristic name

      High Street is the commonest name in {{council_top_names.0.1|int}} of the UK's {{meta.n_councils|int}} councils, Main Street in {{council_top_names.1.1|int}}, Station Road in {{council_top_names.2.1|int}} and Church Lane in {{council_top_names.3.1|int}}. More revealing is each council's signature name: the name, used at least four times there and 25 times in the UK, that is most over-represented compared with the UK as a whole. These are usually the road to the next town (Lynn Road in King's Lynn and West Norfolk), a landscape word (The Loke in North Norfolk) or history (General Wade's Military Road in Highland, Perth and Kinross).

      08 · MethodHow I did this, and what to watch for

      Great Britain. OS Open Names lists every named road in Great Britain as a point, with the town or village it belongs to. I kept the {{meta.n_gb|int}} features of type Named Road. Ordnance Survey also lists sections of named roads inside settlements; those duplicate a road already counted, so I left them out. A Named Road is Ordnance Survey's grouping of road stretches that share a name, so a very long road may appear more than once and two short streets of the same name may be one.

      Northern Ireland. Ordnance Survey's open data stop at Great Britain, so for Northern Ireland I used OpenStreetMap: {{meta.prep.ni_ways_kept|int}} named road ways (residential to trunk roads; no paths, tracks or service roads). OpenStreetMap splits a street into many pieces, so I joined pieces with the same name that share an end or have ends within {{meta.prep.link_m|int}} m, giving {{meta.n_ni|int}} streets, and dropped {{meta.prep.ni_bridges_dropped|int}} short pieces named as bridges. The two sources group streets differently, so I compare shares rather than counts across the Irish Sea.

      Places. Each street is placed in a current council area (ONS local authority districts) by its point, and on 10 km and 20 km squares of the British National Grid. Regions in England are those of Ordnance Survey. For the town and village comparison, each street's Ordnance Survey settlement name was matched to the nearest settlement of that name in OS Open Names, within 8 km.

      Words. The ending is the last word when it is one of {{n_generics|int}} common street words (Road, Lane, Wynd, Loke and so on); otherwise a name has no ending (The Green, Briggate). Exact names (High Street) are counted exactly, so High Road, Upper High Street and Main Road are not included. Welsh names are those Ordnance Survey marks as Welsh, plus names in Wales starting with a Welsh street word. Origins of words in the text are the usual dictionary readings; the data show only where names are, not why.

      Limits. Street names change, and new estates bring names chosen by developers and councils, so this is a map of today's names, not of when they were given. Counts depend on how Ordnance Survey and OpenStreetMap divide streets. OpenStreetMap coverage in Northern Ireland is good on named roads but not complete.
